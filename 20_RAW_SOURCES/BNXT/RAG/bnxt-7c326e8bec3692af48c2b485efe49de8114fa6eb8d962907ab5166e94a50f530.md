# Love them or loathe them? Five electric enduro bikes we’re excited about in 2026

Published on August 26th, 2026 by Enduro21 Team

#### **Leccy bikes are becoming a common sight in paddocks around the world – for better or for worse – if you're thinking of trying one out, here's our pick of the five most interesting.**

Love them or loathe them, electric bikes are becoming impossible to ignore. Some have already embraced them with open arms, heralding the minimal maintenance and near-silent running as game-changing, while others remain dyed-in-the-wool petrol purists.

Here at Enduro21, we sit somewhere in the middle. Internal combustion is still king as far as we're concerned, but EVs are getting closer and ever more manufacturers seem to be coming out of the woodwork promising ground-breaking performance. Here are five of the most exciting electric dirt bikes available now or heading our way very soon.

### **1) The benchmark: Stark Varg EX**

Assuming today is not your first on the internet (congratulations if so), the well-proven Varg EX likely won't need much in the way of introduction.

 Revealed to the world in late 2021 before reaching customers in 2023, the Spanish-built machine has done more than any other motorcycle to change perceptions of what an electric dirt bike can be.

After the original Varg MX came the bike that really grabbed our attention, the homologated EX version, bringing a bigger 7.2kWh battery, lights, and chassis revisions to suit its new job away from the motocross track.

Where ICE enduro bikes are usually softened compared with their closed-course cousins, the Varg EX escapes the same neutering, delivering an almost ridiculous 79bhp and a headline-grabbing 692lb.ft of torque.

Let’s be clear though, and as is the case with most non-combustion bikes, that figure is quoted at the rear wheel, amplified by the internal reduction gearing and final drive rather than measured at the motor itself. Why manufacturers insist on quoting torque that way is beyond us. The motor actually produces 66.4lb.ft – though that's still knocking on double what most 450s manage.

Technology has been a huge part of the Varg's appeal. Riders can choose from hundreds of virtual engine maps through Stark's smartphone app, tailoring everything from engine braking and throttle response to traction control and regenerative braking. One minute it can be an easy-going pussy cat, the next it can unleash performance that eclipses most 450s in full poo-your-pants-and-try-to-hang-on mode.

The formula has proved successful both commercially and competitively. Stark has grown rapidly since launch, expanding production, opening new markets and attracting significant investment as demand continues to increase. In competition, the Varg has become a genuine front-runner, this year nabbing a ninth-place finish at Erzberg at the hands of Eddie Karlsson, while more recently, the Swede rode to a fourth-place finish in front of a home crowd at the inaugural ForzaOrza.

### **Stark Varg EX specifications**

Power: 79bhp (60kW)

Torque: 692 lb.ft (66.4 lb.ft at the motor)

Weight: 120kg

Battery: 7.2kWh

Price: From €12,900 (for the full fat Alpha version)

### 2) **The new king? Radian EXR**

On paper, the Radian EXR solves the biggest issue Stark is yet to figure out – battery swapping.

Due to arrive in its initial markets from the final quarter of 2026, the biggest selling point (for us at least) is the Radian's toolless quick swap battery system, whereby a pair of cassette-type units (each weighing 22kg) can be replaced once depleted in a matter of seconds.

At 8.6kWh combined, the total capacity of the EXR outdoes the biggest battery available on a Varg, and ride time is said to be up to seven hours or 115km in optimal conditions.

The rest of the package looks equally promising. Although a little on the porky side at 125kg, much of that mass can be explained by the extra energy stores. Chassis parts look to be as good as anything you'd find on a mainstream production bike, and as you'd expect, it packs a serious punch with 69bhp on tap.

### **Radian EXR specifications**

Power: 69bhp (52kW)

Torque: 782 lb.ft (Radian only provide wheel output figures)

Weight: 125kg

Battery: 8.6kWh

Price: From €14,750 (plus an additional €3450 for the spare battery kit)

### **3) Newcomers from Down Under: Bonnell 902 and Bonnell 805**

Australia has become the latest country to throw its hat into the electric off-road ring, with Perth-based Bonnell unveiling two full-size dirt bikes.

 The flagship Bonnell 902 develops a claimed 86bhp (65kW) and over 660lb.ft of torque (at the wheel), outdoing even the mighty Varg. While technically full-sized, suspension travel is down a little on the usual we've come to expect, with 285mm of movement available here.

Buyers can choose between a larger 6.6kWh battery for maximum range or a lighter 4.6kWh race battery, both designed to be swappable in a few minutes. The option to run a smaller battery should be an obvious weight saver, ideal for sprint enduros and the like. Bonnell is yet to actually state a running mass figure, but the expectation is that it will undercut the Stark.

Alongside the 902 sits the lighter Bonnell 805, producing a claimed 55bhp (42kW) while retaining the same full-size chassis dimensions, removable battery system, and smartphone-based electronics package.

Both bikes feature highly configurable riding modes, allowing owners to tailor throttle response, power delivery, and regenerative braking through a dedicated app.

### **Bonnell 902 specifications**

Power: 86bhp (65kW)

Torque: 900Nm+

Weight: TBC

Battery: 6.6kWh or 4.6kWh removable

Price: TBC

### **Bonnell 805 specifications**

Power: 55bhp (42kW)

Weight: TBC

Battery: 6.6kWh or 4.6kWh removable

Price: TBC

### **4) The familiar face: KTM Freeride E**

Despite all the newcomers popping up, the big players in the industry don’t appear to have much effort into electric so far – urban runabouts or trials concepts aside. Honda and Yamaha might be churning out small-capacity equivalent scooters by the bucketload, and the big H also has its catchily named WN7 roadster (which let's face it, very few people will actually spend their hard earned on).

We say all the major manufacturers, because of course, there is that one from Austria who started banging the electric off-road drum a while bike, albeit rather quietly.

KTM has been offering a battery-powered almost-enduro bike for over a decade now. When the Freeride E (which is really intended for dicking about on, not competition-type use) first appeared, it was pretty limited as far as power or range were concerned.

Even in its latest guise, where the bike is by far the closest to an EXC it's ever been, performance is still down against the other models on this list.

That said, there's something to be said for the might of KTM's extensive dealer network, and if all you're buying the bike for is to play in the woods, do you really need as much power as a small family hatchback? Utilising a single 29kg 5.5kWh lithium-ion power source, the Freeride E is cheaper and lighter than its rivals at a claimed weight of just 112kg. It's also noticeably smaller overall, too – essentially making it the Beta XTrainer of the electric bike world, and who doesn’t like those?

### **KTM Freeride E specifications**

Power: 69bhp (52kW)

Torque: 27 lb.ft (at the motor)

Weight: 112kg

Battery: 5.5kWh

Price: From £8999

### **5) GOAT approved: Jarv-E**

If anyone can convince us that ditching petrol-power is worth a shout, it's surely got to be the GOAT himself, Graham Jarvis. Living up to his 'Silent Assassin' moniker, Jarvis has now put his World Championship-winning stamp to an EV.

Still in development, the Jarv-E is engineered specifically as a hard enduro tool, rather than starting life as an MX bike. It's physically compact, with just a 900mm seat height, and is heaps lighter than anything else on this list at just 99kg. Although the Jarv-E's appeal will naturally be rather limited, meaning tiny production volumes, those that do make the investment will be protected by a two-year warranty.

Despite being 26kg less weighty than a Varg EX, battery capacity is actually slightly greater, and with less mass to lug around, range ought to be respectable, too. We're yet to throw a leg over the Jarv-E, but Graham or David (the founder behind the project), if you're reading this, you have our email!

### **Jarv-E specifications**

Power: 67bhp (50kW)

Torque: NA

Weight: 99kg

Battery: 7.3kWh

Price: From £10,300

### **The big battery debate**

Despite the progress electric powertrains have made over the past five years, the area where battery bikes continue to fall well behind their dino-fuelled counterparts is range.

Today's batteries are more than adequate for an afternoon in the woods. But once speeds increase – or use times stretch above roughly three hours – electricity falls short.

Solid-state batteries are widely viewed as the next major breakthrough. By replacing the liquid electrolyte (typically a solvent with lithium salts dissolved in it) found in conventional lithium-ion batteries with a solid material, they promise higher energy density, faster charging, lower fire risk and significantly longer lifespan. In simple terms, that should mean lighter bikes capable of going much further between a session on the mains plug.

Automotive giants including Toyota, Nissan, BMW, Mercedes-Benz and Volkswagen-backed companies have collectively invested billions into solid-state development, with the first production cars expected to arrive before the end of the decade.

Keen to bring bikes into the fold, Swedish company Verg announced a world-first range of solid-state-powered two-wheelers, with these batteries produced in partnership with sister company Donut Lab. We're straying away from enduro a little here, but the promised results have big potential away from the road – where the bikes will apparently be good for 370 miles per charge, while 180 miles can be achieved after just 10 minutes hooked up to a DC fast charger.

Realistically, the technology is still a long way away and, let's face it, off-road bikes are unlikely to be at the forefront of it. Nonetheless, if the car makers can crack solid state by 2030, we might not have to wait too long before the tech trickles down, and when it does, that'll solve the biggest limitation leccy bikes face today. Then we just need to figure out how to make them smell and sound good...
