# Tested: Ducati Desmo450 EDS first ride – the new benchmark?

Published on August 17th, 2026 by Nye Davis

#### **Enduro21 heads to Italy's Motor Valley to try out Ducati's highly anticipated debut enduro bike, the Desmo450 EDS. Has the Bologna marque got it nailed from the off?**

A few years ago, you'd have never thought to mix the Ducati name with the niche off-road world. But roll forward to the present day, and the firm now boasts a four-strong range of dedicated machines for just that, including the newly revealed Desmo450 EDS enduro bike – and now we've ridden it.

Back in 2024, while chatting to none other than Mr Tony Cairoli at World Ducati Week, the big dog of the manufacturer's MX racing efforts confirmed in no uncertain terms that a plated version built on the 450MX (the bike which Cairoli was thoroughly mopping up the Italian MX GP with at the time) was in the works.

Now that you or I can buy one, the question arises – is it any good?

After a day spent putting the bike through its paces, the answer to that is a resounding yes. In short, the new kid on the block is definitely one to be taken seriously and a genuine contender against the best that its Japanese or European rivals can muster. In typical Italian fashion, it's not hard to look at either, and the build quality looks every bit that of the equivalent Yamaha or Honda.

#### **It's not a converted MX bike**

Okay, it actually sort of is, but rather than taking its resident motocrosser and throwing on a smaller back wheel and a headlight, Ducati has put in the development hours here to come up with every bit a true, sorted, out-the-box enduro machine - a bloody good one at that. That much is obvious as soon as you thumb the starter button.

#### **Reworked motor**

While the basic architecture of the 449.6cc powerplant carries over from the 450 MX, the smaller 42mm throttle body, EDS-specific mapping, milder cam profile, reduced compression ratio, tweaked exhaust, and increased crankshaft and flywheel mass – creating nearly 30% more inertia – all adding up to a tangible real-world difference.

Toggle the map-switch into the softer 'green' map and the EDS is friendly and forgiving, if a little muted when prodded hard. The spicier 'red' setting serves up additional bark, providing a more direct response, and for those in need of further oomph, there's the option to add a titanium slip-on or full system from Akrapovič. Interestingly, Ducati's resident enduro ace Antoine Meo was present while we were testing, and the five-time World Champion was running the slip-on only on his bike (not the full system) – take from that what you will...

Fuelling is glitch-free through the rev range and while it'll obviously never lug like a two-stroke, the bike only flamed out on one occasion during our whole day's riding. It'll pull from right down near idle, and thanks to the Desmo valvetrain, there's a huge amount of rpm to play with up top. All big ticks so far, Ducati!

There's nothing to grumble about where the transmission is concerned, either. The clutch pull from the Brembo master cylinder is positive and the ratios in the six-speed gearbox are well spaced – although we'd probably shorten the final drive a touch if we had hold of the bike for longer. The quickshifter works a treat, too, though you'd expect it'd be squared away from the off given how long Ducati has been fitting these to its road catalogue.

#### **Chassis performance**

Hard-charging cross-country types will love the stiff aluminium frame set-up taken from the motocross bike, though (for me at least) the chassis never quite had the instantly familiar handling of, say, a KTM (or white/red equivalent), Beta or Sherco. I also haven't been on a four stroke in the best part of a year, so there will be an element of rustiness clouding thoughts here.

That said, the suspension itself gets a big tick and getting things dialled took only **a** few small tweaks. The Showa forks and shock might be largely the same as those used on the 450 MX, but revised spring rates, damping settings, and an additional oil bypass hole in the rear shock piston make for a package that's dialled beautifully for the job at hand.

#### **Clever electrics**

In base trim, rider aids and engine characteristics are not tuneable beyond the presets built into the two standard maps. Chuck the £177.98 Wi-Fi module onto your order, however, and there opens up heaps of choice to tailor throttle response, engine braking, and traction control to suit individual needs and wants - all accessible via an easy-to-use, free phone app which can also handily suggest suspension settings for rider size, experience, as well as track conditions, and more.

As a side note, the LED unit up front on the Desmo throws out lumens better than any stock unit we've seen, kudos, Ducati.

#### **Any niggles?**

The bike is mightily impressive, given that the Desmo is a first-year model, and writing this section is requiring some serious clutching at straws. That said, no bike is perfect.

In future versions of the bike, we're hoping to see Ducati sort out that bulging fuel tank. The Desmo valving simply needs more room than a conventional sprung design, that's a fact, and with it there's simply less space for the bike's 8.5-litre petrol capacity. Nicking the smaller tank would obviously shrink things down (but doing so requires using the MX rad shrouds and seat), although that doesn't sound like a viable solution, really – perhaps a rear-mounted TM-style design is the way to go here, Ducati?

#### **A word from the champ**

It's all well and good giving our opinion, but when Mr Speedy Bollocks himself, AKA David Knight, is parked next to you in the pits, we'd be daft not to get his lowdown on the bike. Between laps, Enduro21 borrowed five minutes from the multiple-time British and World Champion. Here's what he had to say.

You've spent plenty of time on the Desmo450 MX and have done a fair bit to turn it into an enduro bike, how does the EDS compare?

*"It's hard to know what to expect. With this, obviously you hear they've fitted a heavier crankshaft and things like that, which is what you want to hear, but until you ride it you don't know if it's going to be the same as the motocross bike or if it's going to be too much the other way.*

*Jumping on it, you know if a bike's good within the first couple of minutes, even the first few corners. Even when I started it up, I thought, 'oh good, it's mellow.' It's not slow, but it's mellow.*

*The real test is getting out of a corner, getting on the gas, then overshooting the next corner because you don't realise how fast you're actually going.*

*I started on the green map, did a couple of laps just to get a feel for it, then switched to the faster map, which was way better for me. It just gave it that bit of grunt.*

*I was surprised by how easy it is to ride, which is what I love. I know Antoine [Meo] has done a lot of work on the bike, and he has the same philosophy I believe in. I'd rather have a bike that feels a bit slow than one that's too fast, because you can always make them go faster. It's much harder to calm a motocross bike down properly.*

*I was riding pretty much everywhere in third and fourth gear. I tried to avoid second and just keep it in a tall gear. I could go into really tight corners in third gear, let it lug and pull out, and it goes well. But it's not explosive power where it's spinning and going lock to lock.*

*Chassis-wise, the bike feels very good because the engine is helping it. I know from my own bike that when you've got extra power, you can have massive moments if it flicks off a root or something. With this, I felt confident getting on the throttle mid-corner and carrying speed through it. They've done a bloody good job. I could stick stiffer springs in it and race it as it is."*

#### **Where to buy one**

Priced at £11,245 in the UK, $12,995 in the US, or €12,990 in Italy, the debut machine is now beginning to trickle into European showrooms (the rest of the world to follow shortly).

At the moment, just six specialist sites with specially trained technicians across the UK will stock the EDS and its MX sibling models, and it's a similar story globally. You can't get the bikes, spares or servicing from any other Ducati dealer.

For more info and to find your nearest dealership, visit: [www.ducati.com](http://www.ducati.com/)
